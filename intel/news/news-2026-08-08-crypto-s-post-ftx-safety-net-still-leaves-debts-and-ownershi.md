Four years after FTX's collapse, major crypto platforms continue to publish proof-of-reserves dashboards that verify control over selected wallets while leaving the full picture of liabilities and corporate structure unexamined. The CryptoSlate analysis shows these tools confirm that certain customer balances appear in a dataset and that the exchange signed messages from listed addresses, yet they provide no guarantee that every customer is included or that the assets are unencumbered.

## What the verifications actually establish

A user who runs a Merkle-tree check receives cryptographic confirmation that their account balance was part of the dataset at one moment in time. The same process demonstrates that the exchange controlled the private keys to the published wallet addresses on that date. CryptoSlate notes that this evidence is technically sound for those narrow claims but stops short of addressing whether the displayed coins have been pledged as collateral, whether obligations are owed by affiliates, or whether the company actually controlling the wallets matches the entity legally required to repay the customer.

Early post-FTX efforts, prompted by Binance founder Changpeng Zhao, focused on publishing wallet addresses and reserve ratios. Those dashboards reassured many participants because cryptographic outputs carry more perceived weight than corporate statements. The limitation, according to the reporting, is that the outputs only capture assets at a fixed point without mapping the broader balance sheet.

## Gaps that remain unaddressed

No common standard governs which entities, assets, or obligations a reserve page must cover. As a result, one exchange's report may exclude liabilities, loans, legal claims, or obligations owed by affiliates. The analysis states that users cannot determine from the public interface whether the company actually controlling the wallet is the same company legally required to repay the customer.

Reserve checks also omit any view of liabilities, loans, or legal claims. Four years after the FTX failure, the industry has not converged on a verification method that combines asset attestation with liability disclosure.

## Evidence limitations in the current record

The CryptoSlate article supplies the primary documentation for these shortcomings, drawing on the post-FTX rollout of Merkle-tree tools and the absence of standardized liability reporting. No additional independent datasets or exchange audits appear in the available material to quantify how many platforms still omit key obligations. This leaves the scale of the gap dependent on the single detailed examination provided.

Readers should examine each exchange's reserve page for explicit statements on which legal entities are included and whether any pledged collateral or affiliate debts are disclosed. Cross-reference wallet addresses against on-chain data and review corporate filings where available to identify unreported obligations. Watch for any platform that begins publishing combined asset-liability attestations signed by auditors, as that step would directly test the concerns raised in the current reporting.
